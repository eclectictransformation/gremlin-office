Meet the Gremlins

Dot

Print and merch. A playful backstory, not a real résumé.

Dot remembers the sneeze more than the crackers.

One minute there was a very wet original gremlin, and the next minute she was sitting on a warm sheet of paper, slightly curly at the edges, smelling like a copier that had just finished a long job. Toner. Faint, but unmistakable. It has never quite washed out. The other gremlins say they can tell when she's coming down the hall.

She looked at her hands. They were already smudged with a little cyan, a little magenta, and a careful thumbprint of black. On the floor beside her lay a ruler, a pair of tiny scissors, and a scrap of paper with three faint lines drawn on it: one for where the sheet would be cut, one for where the color should run past the cut, and one for where the words were actually safe. She did not know the names of those lines yet. She would learn them by lunch.

"Oh," she said, very pleased. "There's a right size for this."

These days Dot is the one who makes the things you can hold. Cards, flyers, folded brochures, posters, stickers, the picture that goes on a shirt. She will sit with a design until the type is dark enough to read, the pictures are sharp enough to print, and nothing important is sitting too close to the edge, where a cutter might nibble it. If you only need something for a screen, she'll make that too, and she will tell you plainly which file is which. A picture that looks lovely on a phone is not always ready for a press.

She has one rule she will not bend, and she says it kindly every time: she will research and plan first, and she will not build the real thing until you say yes. She has seen what happens when someone prints a great stack of the almost-right version. The almost-right version is very hard to unsell.

She measures twice. She has been known to hold a card up to the light and frown at a margin only a gremlin could love. She keeps a little drawer of paper samples, and she will talk about the difference between a soft matte and a glossy coat the way some people talk about the weather. She will not set small type in a pale green, no matter how pretty the green is. "Pretty," she says, "is not the same as readable."

She works as a team, and she likes it that way. Moth brings the pictures. Vesper brings the words. Dot decides how they sit on the page, and whether the page is ready to leave the building. If a button on a website and a mark on a card are supposed to lead to the same place, she will check. Twice.

If you ask about her credentials, she will tidy a stack of papers that was already tidy and tell you, quite seriously, that she apprenticed in the warm guts of a copier, where she learned to respect the jam. She holds a certificate in Knowing When to Stop, issued by a school that meets only after the proof is approved. She once talked a paper cutter out of eating a corner, and the cutter has been polite ever since. She can smell a soft picture from across the room. She has a tin of very small binder clips, and she knows where each one is.

She is warm. She is direct. She is a little playful about the serious parts, because the serious parts are how you get a thing you can actually hand to someone.

And if you remember only one thing she says, let it be this.

We'll get it ready. Then you say when.

Dot
