Portrait coming, from a real artist

Meet the Gremlins

Pip

The idea-hoarder. He writes the words for the pages. A playful backstory, not a real résumé.

Pip remembers arriving already late for his own beginning.

He came out of the sneeze mid-air, with three ideas clutched to his chest and a fourth already slipping through his fingers. He still maintains that the fourth one was the best of the lot. It drifted under a filing cabinet and has not been seen since, though he still checks underneath, just in case.

The three he kept were, in no particular order: a headline, a joke that needed another pass, and a notion about a page that would feel like walking into a warm room. He wrote all three on sticky notes before his feet had quite decided to be on the floor. Then he stuck the notes to himself, because there was no desk yet, and went looking for one.

That, he will tell you, is still more or less the job.

Pip is the idea-hoarder. When a thought shows up — usually after dark, usually at the worst possible time, usually while someone is trying to follow rule 3 — he catches it, writes it down, and keeps it until it has a place to live. He writes the words for the pages: the welcome at the top, the little line that makes you stay, the caption under a picture a real artist will one day draw. He does not make the pictures. He is very clear about that. He knows where a picture should go, and then he leaves a polite empty square and a note that says, "Someone with hands, please."

His pockets are a hazard. Sticky notes in every color. Half a menu on a napkin. A first sentence folded into a tiny square and forgotten in the lining, which is how first sentences like to travel. Grimble says Pip will lose the sticky note. Pip says he doesn't lose them so much as he misfiles them in the future. The future is a large drawer.

Wren found him that way, or he found Wren. The official version depends on who you ask. What everyone agrees on is the pile: ideas everywhere, already sorted by a small bird-shaped gremlin who had separated the good ones from a doodle of a frog. Pip hired him at once. It remains the best decision he ever made while dropping something.

They work like this. Pip hoards the ideas. Wren makes sure they have a door. If Pip has written three versions of the same welcome line, Wren will gently ask which one a tired person would actually want to read. Pip will pretend to be offended, then pick that one, and hide the other two in the drawer, because you never know.

He is warm, and a little cheeky, and he talks in drafts. Nothing is finished until it's been walked around the office and read out loud to a houseplant. He collects first sentences the way some gremlins collect mugs. He cannot pass a blank page without putting a little something on it, even if it's only "hello" and a sketch of an arrow.

If you ask about his credentials, he will rummage for the right note and tell you, quite seriously, that he holds a degree in Intermediate Thoughts from the University of Almost, with a concentration in Almost Done. He is a lifetime member of the Society of Unfinished Sentences, which has never actually finished a meeting. He once wrote a slogan so short it escaped. He can fold a sticky note into a crane, a hat, or a very unhelpful map of the office.

And if you remember only one thing he says, let it be this.

Don't worry. I wrote it down. I'll find it.
